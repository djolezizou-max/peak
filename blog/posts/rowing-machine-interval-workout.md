You sat down on the rower wanting something simple: a minute hard, a minute easy, eight times. The monitor can do it, in theory. In practice it is a run of menu presses for work time, rest time and number of intervals, and by the time it is set up your warm-up is over and your legs have gone cold.

Then the session starts and the second problem shows up. The flywheel is loud, the gym music is louder, and the phone beep you were relying on disappears under both. So you keep glancing at the monitor, which breaks your rhythm at the exact moment you are trying to hold it.

## Pick time pieces or distance pieces, not both

Rowing intervals come in two shapes. Time pieces are fixed blocks: 1 minute on, 1 minute off. Distance pieces are fixed lengths: 8 x 500 metres with 2 minutes of rest. Both work, but they are run differently, and mixing them up is where most setups go wrong.

Time pieces are the natural fit for a timer. The work block ends on the clock whatever your pace, so a timer can run the whole session and the monitor only has to show your split. Most HIIT on the rower should be built this way: 30 to 90 seconds of work, because the rower punishes long all-out efforts quickly.

Distance pieces end when the monitor says so, which means you finish on a number you have to look at. The easiest approach is to let the monitor count the metres and let a timer run the fixed rest between them. If you know your split, you can also convert: at a 2:00 split, 500 metres is two minutes, so a timed 2-minute piece gets you close enough for training without ever looking down.

## Give each block a stroke rate, not just an effort

On a rower, effort without a stroke rate turns into flailing. People sprint by rating up to 36 strokes a minute with short, weak strokes and get slower, not faster. The fix is to give every block a target rate and keep the power in the legs.

A good default is 26 to 30 strokes a minute for work and 18 to 20 for recovery. Recovery is a real paddle, not stopping: light pressure, long slow strokes, breathing settled enough to say a short sentence. If your hard split drifts more than 3 or 4 seconds per 500 metres slower across the session, the work blocks are too long or the rate is too high.

Set the damper between 3 and 5 and leave it there. A damper of 10 feels harder, but it mostly makes each stroke heavier and slower, and it fatigues your back long before your heart and lungs get the work you came for.

## A 30-minute rowing machine interval session

This works on a Concept2, a Hydrow or the unbranded rower at a hotel gym:

1. Five minutes easy at 20 strokes a minute, adding a few hard strokes in the last minute.
2. Ten seconds of prep to sit at the catch and take the handle.
3. Ten rounds of 1 minute hard at 28 strokes a minute and 1 minute easy paddle at 18.
4. Four minutes of cooldown, easy pressure, rate falling to 16.

That is 29 minutes and every decision is made before you start. In week one, do eight rounds instead of ten. Once ten rounds feel controlled, move to 90 seconds on and 60 off rather than rating higher. The next step after that is a pyramid of 1, 2, 3, 2, 1 minutes hard with a minute of paddle after each, which is tedious to program on most monitors and trivial on a saved timer.

## Hear the change over the flywheel

On a rower you want your eyes forward and your posture tall, so the best cue is one you do not have to look for. A spoken "paddle" a couple of seconds before the work block ends lets you finish the stroke you are on and ease off cleanly. A round count read out loud, "round seven of ten", is what lets you pace the back half instead of guessing.

## Two rates and a timer

The rower rewards structure more than intensity. Decide whether today is time pieces or distance pieces, give each block a stroke rate, keep the damper sensible, and let a timer you can hear tell you when to switch.

Do that and the monitor goes back to being a split display, which is what it is good at. The only thing you change week to week is how many rounds you row or how long each one lasts.
