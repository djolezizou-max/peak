You want a countdown that beeps when work ends and beeps when rest ends. That is a solved problem, and paying £5 a month forever for it feels absurd. So you search for a free timer, install the top three results, and discover that "free" means something different in each of them.

The catch is rarely on day one. A free tier is designed to feel complete for the first workout and to run out somewhere in the second week, once you have built a real routine. Knowing which limits bite, and when, is the whole buying decision.

## The four ways a free tier runs out

**Saved workout caps.** The most common limit, and the sneakiest. Two or three saved workouts sounds like plenty until you have a Tabata session, a 40/20 circuit, a treadmill protocol and a warm-up. Then every new session means deleting an old one or rebuilding it by hand.

**Watch and device access.** Many timers run free on the phone and charge for the Apple Watch or Wear OS app. If the reason you want a timer is to leave the phone on the bench, this is the limit that matters most, and it is often buried in a feature list rather than the price.

**Custom structure and cues.** Free tiers usually allow a simple work/rest/rounds timer. Ladders, multiple blocks, named exercises, separate prep and cooldown phases, and per-phase sounds tend to sit behind the paywall. That is fine for straight Tabata and frustrating for anything with shape.

**Ads at the wrong moment.** Ad-supported timers are free in money and expensive in attention. An interstitial at the round change, or a banner that shifts the start button, costs you more than it saves the developer.

## Subscription, one-off, or ad-supported

There are really three pricing models, and each suits a different person.

**Ad-supported free** suits someone running the same simple timer two or three times a week who never looks at the screen mid-set. If you do look, test the app through a full workout before trusting it, because ads rarely show up in the first sixty seconds.

**Monthly subscription** makes sense if you are trying a structured programme for a couple of months and want everything unlocked without committing. Most offer a free trial; set a calendar reminder the day before it converts.

**One-off purchase** is the right answer for most people who train intervals for more than a season. A timer is a utility you will use for years, and the maths is simple: if the one-time price is less than a year of the subscription, and you expect to still be training next year, paying once wins.

## How to check before you install

The App Store and Google Play listings both show an "In-App Purchases" section. Open it before installing. A list with only monthly and yearly prices tells you there is no way to pay once. A "Lifetime" or "Unlock" entry tells you there is.

Then read the screenshots for words like "Pro", "Premium" or a small lock icon. Developers almost always show the paid features in the screenshots, which makes the screenshots a reliable map of what the free tier does not include.

Finally, build your real week in the first session — every workout you actually do — rather than the one quick timer the onboarding suggests. If a cap exists, you will hit it in five minutes instead of discovering it on a Thursday morning with your shoes already on.

## The short version

Free timers are genuinely fine for one simple protocol. The moment you want several saved sessions, a Watch, or custom phases, you are going to pay something — so look for an app that lets you pay once, check the In-App Purchases list before installing, and build your full week on day one to find the cap early.
