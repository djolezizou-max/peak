Apple added custom intervals to the Workout app, and the honest reaction from anyone who already owned a timer app was: do I still need this? It is a fair question. The stock feature is free, it is already on your wrist, it records a real workout to Fitness, and it taps you at every phase change.

So the useful answer is not "get an app." It is a list of the specific places the built-in version stops being enough — because for a lot of sessions it never does, and paying attention to another icon is a waste.

## What the built-in Workout app genuinely does well

If your session is a flat repeat — forty seconds on, twenty off, twelve rounds, done — the Workout app covers it completely. You build the custom workout on the iPhone in the Fitness app, it syncs to the Watch, and it runs with haptic cues and a proper rings-and-heart-rate record at the end.

That last part is the real advantage, and no third-party timer beats it outright. A stock custom workout is a first-class Apple workout: it lands in your activity history with the correct type, GPS if it is outdoors, and heart rate zones you can scroll through afterwards. If you care most about the log, start here.

It also handles the two things people most often assume they need an app for: a warmup block and a cooldown block, and distance- or calorie-based steps rather than pure time. A run of "five minutes warmup, then eight rounds of 400 metres hard and 90 seconds easy" is entirely inside what the stock builder can do.

## Where it runs out

**Nested structure.** The built-in builder gives you a flat sequence with one repeat. The moment your session is "five rounds of 60/60, then three minutes rest, and do that whole block three times," you are back to entering thirty-three steps by hand, because there is no repeat inside a repeat. This is the single most common wall people hit, and it is a builder limitation, not a timing one.

**Per-phase cues.** Every phase change in the stock app feels the same. If you are running a ladder where work intervals change length — 60, 45, 30, 20 — you cannot make the "this one is short, go harder" boundary sound or feel different from an ordinary rest. You end up glancing at the wrist to find out which phase you just entered, which is exactly the thing a cue is meant to prevent.

**Reuse and editing.** Custom workouts live in Fitness on the iPhone. Changing one interval means picking up your phone, editing, and waiting for the sync. If you tweak a session week to week — most people running structured intervals do — that round trip gets old fast.

**Cues that quietly stop.** The most-reported friction is not a missing feature at all: people timing yoga holds or lifting rest report the interval haptics silently dying partway through a session, a couple of times a month, with no warning that the workout is still nominally running. When your only signal is a tap you are not consciously watching for, a failure looks exactly like a long interval, and you find out by counting in your head or staring at your wrist mid-pose.

**Automation.** You cannot reliably launch a specific custom Workout-app interval from a Shortcut. If your training routine starts with a tap on a home screen automation or an NFC tag by the rack, the stock builder is a dead end.

## The honest decision rule

Use the built-in Workout app when the session is a flat repeat, you want it in your Fitness history with zero fuss, and you rarely change it. That covers a genuinely large share of interval training, and there is no prize for over-tooling it.

Reach for a dedicated timer when the structure has nesting, when you want different cues for different phases, when you build and edit sessions often, or when you want to start the thing from an automation rather than four taps into the Workout app.

Each phase carries its own audio and haptic cue, so a short work interval announces itself differently from a rest. There is a dedicated preparation phase before the first rep, which is the one boundary you are actually standing still for. And workouts are saved objects you edit in seconds and re-run from the wrist, rather than iPhone documents that sync over.

## What most people actually end up doing

Both, and that is a reasonable outcome rather than a fence-sit. Plenty of people run their steady, repeatable sessions through the stock Workout app for the clean Fitness record, and pull up a timer app for the structured or experimental days — the ladders, the nested blocks, the EMOM variations, the mobility circuits where every hold is a different length.

If that is you, the one thing worth doing is deciding in advance which session belongs to which, instead of rebuilding the same workout in both places. Put the flat repeats in Fitness. Put anything with a shape in the timer app, and name it so it is one tap away next week.

The built-in intervals are good. They are just built for the simple case, and structured training stops being the simple case quickly.
