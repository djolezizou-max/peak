Your workout is written down as three lines. Five rounds of one minute hard, one minute easy. Three minutes of recovery. Do that whole thing three times. It took ten seconds to write and it should take ten seconds to program.

Instead you are sitting on the gym floor tapping in thirty-three separate intervals, because the timer you downloaded only understands a flat list. This is the single most common complaint about interval timers, and it has nothing to do with the timing itself — the timing is fine. It is the builder that gives up as soon as your session has structure.

## The problem is nesting, not length

A flat timer asks for work, rest, and a round count. That covers 20/10 Tabata and it covers 40/20 circuits, and for a lot of people that is genuinely enough.

It falls apart the moment one repeat sits inside another. A running ladder — 5 × (1 min hard / 1 min easy), then 3 min recovery, all repeated 3 times — is two levels deep. So is a CrossFit-style session with three different EMOM blocks separated by rest, or a boxing workout where three-minute rounds cycle through different drills. The pattern is the same: a group of intervals that repeats as a unit.

Flatten that by hand and 3 lines become 33. Then you change your mind about the recovery length and you get to do it again.

## Build the group, then repeat the group

The fix is to treat a set as a thing you can name and reuse, not a range you highlight. In Peak Interval you build the inner block once — one minute hard, one minute easy — set its repeat count to 5, append the 3-minute recovery, and then wrap that whole sequence in an outer repeat of 3.

Three entries instead of thirty-three. More importantly, editing works the way you actually think: drop the hard efforts to 45 seconds and every one of the fifteen updates at once, because there is only one of them.

The same shape covers most structured sessions. A pyramid is one block whose durations step up and back down. A HYROX simulation is a run block and a station block alternating. A strength-and-conditioning finisher is a heavy block, a rest, and a light block, repeated until the clock runs out.

## Different work-to-rest ratios in one session

Repeating sets solve the second half of the problem too: blocks that are not identical.

Most people who train seriously do not run one ratio for a whole session. You might open with 4 × 30/30 to raise heart rate, move to 6 × 40/20 as the main work, and close with 8 × 20/10 when form is already going. Three blocks, three ratios, one continuous workout — and with a flat timer, three separate timers you have to stop and restart between.

Stopping between blocks is not a cosmetic annoyance. It is fifteen seconds of fumbling with a phone while your heart rate drops and the interval you were supposed to be resting through turns into two minutes. Sequencing the blocks inside one saved workout means you press start once and never touch the phone again.

## Make it run without the screen

A structured workout is only as good as the cues that get you through it, because the whole point is that you are not looking at anything.

That means the audio has to tell you where you are, not just that something changed. A distinct cue for work versus rest, a countdown into each transition, and — the one that matters most in a nested workout — a marker when the outer set rolls over, so you know you are starting round two of three rather than losing count somewhere in the middle. On Apple Watch the same structure runs on your wrist with haptics doing that job, which is what you want when you are outdoors or wearing headphones playing something loud.

Prep time earns its place here too. Give yourself ten or fifteen seconds at the top so you can put the phone down and get to your mat before the first work interval fires.

## Save it, then stop rebuilding it

Once a session is built as nested blocks, save it. That is the real payoff — the ladder you programmed in October is still there in March, and running it again is one tap.

It also makes progression honest. Keep the structure fixed and change one variable: bump the outer repeat from 3 to 4, or shave the recovery from 3 minutes to 2:30. When only one number moved, you know what caused the workout to feel harder.

If you are choosing a timer right now, this is the feature worth testing before anything else. Open the builder and try to enter your most complicated real workout. If you can describe it in three lines and enter it in three lines, you have the right app. If you find yourself typing the same interval out five times, you will be doing that every week from here.
