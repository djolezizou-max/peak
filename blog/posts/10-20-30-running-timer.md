The minute on paper is simple. Thirty seconds easy, twenty moderate, ten all-out, and you do that five times without stopping. On the track it is not, because all three paces live inside the same sixty seconds, and a normal timer has no name for the middle one.

By the third minute you are counting the sprint in your head. The easy jog runs long, the moderate twenty gets swallowed, and the walk that belongs after the fifth sprint arrives whenever you remember it. A work-and-rest beep cannot sort that out. This run has three efforts, then a break between the five-minute blocks.

## What one minute of 10-20-30 actually is

[Runlovers](https://runlovers.it/en/2026/10-20-30-method-running-interval-workout/) splits the minute into 30 seconds easy, 20 seconds moderate and 10 seconds all-out. Easy is a conversational jog, or a brisk walk if you are cooked. Moderate is where full sentences get hard; they put it near 5K or 10K effort. The ten seconds is the fastest you can run today, and then you drop straight back to easy. Repeat that minute five times without a stop. That is one five-minute block. Walk or jog very slowly for about two minutes, then do the block again. Their session is a 10-minute easy warm-up, three of those blocks (four if you already handle the work), and 3 to 5 minutes easy at the end.

[Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/10-20-30-interval-training-runners-2991502-2/), writing up the University of Copenhagen method, uses the same minute: 30 slow, 20 moderate, 10 as a sprint, repeated three to five times, then a break of one to four minutes. Beginners start with a single five-minute block. Runners who already train start with two blocks and a 3 to 4 minute break. You add blocks, up to four, and shorten the break once the shape is familiar. Do it at least twice a week, and not more than twice at first if you are new to it.

[Runner's World](https://www.runnersworld.com/training/a45783624/10-20-30-workout-running-performance-study/) describes that same minute — 30 seconds low, 20 moderate, 10 high, five times — in a performance study. Futurity's account of the study is the specific one: 19 runners replaced their normal training with 10-20-30 workouts, three or four of the five-minute blocks, three times a week for six weeks. Half were told to take the 10-second piece at about 80 percent rather than all-out. Over 5K, that group was 42 seconds faster on average. The all-out group was 24 seconds faster. Both improved maximum oxygen uptake by 7 percent. Runner's World reports those same 5K and oxygen-uptake changes. The third pace does not have to be a maximum sprint to count as the session. It still has to be its own named block, or you will not know when it started.

## A work and rest pair cannot say which pace you are in

Most interval timers ask for work, rest and a round count. That builds equal hard and easy repeats, which is a different run: [interval running workout timer](/blog/posts/interval-running-workout-timer). It also builds a walk-run ladder, two paces whose ratio changes by the week: [walk-run intervals](/blog/posts/walk-run-interval-timer). Neither of those is three efforts inside one minute.

Force 10-20-30 into work and rest and you have to drop one of the three paces, then count it yourself. The one people count is the sprint, because ten seconds is the piece they are afraid of missing. The 30 and the 20 become whatever is left of the minute. A beep every ten seconds is worse. You are now remembering whether that beep was still easy, the start of moderate, or the sprint.

The minute needs three labels: easy, moderate, sprint. The five-minute block is that minute, five times, with nothing between the minutes. The walk is a fourth label, and it comes after the fifth sprint, not inside the minute.

## Build the five-minute block once

In Peak Interval the inner block is three named intervals: Easy at 0:30, Moderate at 0:20, Sprint at 0:10. Set that group's repeat to 5. Do not put a rest between those minutes. The five minutes are continuous. Typing the fifteen intervals by hand is the same clock, and the first time you change the sprint you have to change it in five places. One group, repeated, is one edit. That nesting is the same shape as [repeating sets](/blog/posts/interval-timer-repeating-sets): build the group, append what comes after it, then repeat the whole thing.

The walk is what you append. For the Runlovers session it is 2:00, and the outer repeat is 3. For the Futurity start for people who already run, the walk is 3:00 or 4:00 and the outer repeat is 2. A beginner does not use the outer repeat. One pass of the five-minute group, then stop.

Keep the warm-up and the cool-down in the same saved workout. Ten minutes easy in front, named Easy so the cue does not say "work", and five minutes easy at the end. Runlovers finishes with 3 to 5 minutes; five is the long end of that range, and it is one number you do not have to remember. A preparation phase of about 15 seconds at the front is enough to zip the phone away before the warm-up starts. If the warm-up lives on a watch and the intervals live in another app, the first sprint starts cold.

Save two workouts, not a new one on the morning of the run.

1. **One block.** 15 seconds of prep, 10:00 easy, one pass of Easy 0:30 / Moderate 0:20 / Sprint 0:10 repeated five times, then 5:00 easy. This is the Futurity beginner start: a single five-minute period.
2. **Three blocks.** The same warm-up, then the five-minute group plus a 2:00 walk, three times, then the 5:00 easy. That is the Runlovers layout. When two blocks and a longer break is the right step, duplicate this workout, set the outer repeat to 2, and set the walk to 3:00 or 4:00.

The three-block version also walks for two minutes after the last sprint, before the cool-down. Leave that walk in. A special last round just to delete it makes the final block a different workout, and the extra easy is where you wanted to be anyway.

## Hear the name of the pace

Three paces in one minute only work if they do not sound the same. One beep means something changed. You still have to remember whether that change was moderate, the sprint, or the walk. Name the intervals Easy, Moderate, Sprint and Walk, and have the timer say the name. Two words is as much as you will catch once the sprint starts.

Ten seconds is short enough that a late cue eats it. A spoken countdown at the end of the moderate 20 puts you on the sprint as it starts. The same countdown at the end of the sprint drops you back to easy, instead of carrying the fast pace into the next 30.

Run one minute of the block at home before you take it outside. Lock the phone, put it in the pocket you will run with, and play whatever you listen to. You should be able to tell Easy from Moderate from Sprint with your eyes closed. On an Apple Watch, start the saved workout from the wrist. The tap at each change is what you still feel when wind or music covers the phone, and the watch face does not have to be readable for you to know which pace you are in.

## The takeaway

Pick the version before you leave: one five-minute block, or three blocks with a two-minute walk. Build the minute as Easy 0:30, Moderate 0:20 and Sprint 0:10, repeat it five times with no gap, and give the walk its own name after the fifth sprint. Save it. On the track the only decision left is the pace the cue just called.
