An AMRAP looks like it needs the simplest timer there is. One countdown, twelve minutes, go. Your phone's built-in timer can do that, so it is fair to ask why anyone would pick a dedicated AMRAP timer at all.

The answer shows up around minute eight. You have lost count of your rounds, you have no idea whether you are on pace, and the alarm that ends the workout is the first thing the clock has told you since you pressed start. A good AMRAP timer is not the one with the most modes. It is the one that answers those two questions while you are still moving.

## The countdown is the easy part

Every timer app can count down from twelve minutes, so do not choose on that. What separates them is what happens around the countdown. Look for a prep phase you can set, ten to fifteen seconds, so pressing start and picking up the kettlebell are not the same moment. Look for a final countdown in the last few seconds, so the end of the cap does not arrive as a surprise halfway through a rep you then cannot count.

Then check that the cap is easy to read from where you will actually be. A phone flat on the floor three metres away shows you a grey smudge. Big digits help, a screen that does not auto-lock mid-workout helps more, and a timer on your wrist helps most.

## Checkpoints beat one long block

The feature that matters most, and that most basic timers lack, is a cue partway through. A twelve-minute AMRAP built as one block tells you nothing until it ends. The same cap split into three four-minute phases, with no rest between them, gives you a cue at minute four and minute eight without any arithmetic. Each cue is a pacing check: on target, behind, or ahead and needing to settle.

So when you compare timers, check whether you can chain several work phases back to back with zero rest, and whether each phase change gets its own sound. Some apps only let you build work-rest pairs, which forces a rest phase you then have to set to one second and ignore. Others offer a dedicated AMRAP mode that is really one countdown with a nicer label. Neither gives you checkpoints.

A final-minute warning is the same idea at a smaller scale. Ideally the last phase is its own block, so the cue that starts it doubles as the signal to stop holding back.

## Who counts the rounds

This is the question people most often get wrong when choosing an AMRAP timer. Some WOD-style apps add a button you tap at the end of each round, and the app keeps the tally and your split times. That is genuinely useful if you will actually tap it, which means the phone or watch has to be within reach at the end of every round, with chalky hands, while breathing hard.

Many people get more reliable counts from something physical: a strip of tape and a marker, a stack of poker chips moved one at a time, or chalk marks on the floor next to the last movement. These never miss a tap and never sit in the wrong pocket. Whatever you pick, decide before you start and do not try to keep the count in your head. By minute nine almost nobody can say for sure whether that was round seven or eight.

## What to check before you choose

Run a five-minute test AMRAP with the timer before you trust it with a benchmark. Can you set a prep phase? Can you split the cap into checkpoint phases with no rest between them, and does each one make a sound you can hear across the room? Is there a final countdown? Can you see the time left from your wrist or across the floor? Does the workout save, so next month's attempt runs on exactly the same clock?

Then decide who counts the rounds, the app or a stack of chips, before the clock starts. A timer that gets those things right is the best AMRAP timer for you, whatever else it has on the feature list.
