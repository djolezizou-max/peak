You hear the tone and still do not know whether the squat just ended or the push-up just started. The phone is on the mat. You look down, read the screen, and get back into position a second late. Then you do it again on the next round.

A beep only marks that a phase changed. Once the rounds are different exercises, the missing piece is a few words: which move is next, and how close the change is.

## Use a beep when every round is the same move

Eight rounds of the same exercise, 20 seconds on and 10 off, is a beep's kind of workout. You already know the movement. You only need the boundary, and a single tone can carry that.

A circuit cannot. Goblet squat, push-up, and rest all sound identical if the only cue is a beep, so you end up watching the phone on a session you started so you would not have to. Voice carries the name of the phase. The on-screen version of that name, for a class that can see a display, is a separate question, covered in [interval timer that shows exercise names](/blog/posts/interval-timer-that-shows-exercise-names). This setup is for the round where your eyes stay on the floor.

## Call the next exercise during the rest

This is the cue that replaces the glance. You want the upcoming move spoken while rest is still running, with enough time to step to the mat, pick up the kettlebell, or switch sides before work starts. A name that arrives as the work interval begins is already late. You spend the opening of the effort getting set.

Peak Interval speaks the label you put on the phase. If the next interval should be announced as "push-up", that word has to be the name of the interval, not a reminder you meant to keep in your head. Keep the label to one or two words. "Squat" fits in a breath. "Kettlebell goblet squat, chest tall, elbows in" does not, and the voice will still try to read all of it.

Put the coaching detail somewhere else. Foot position, tempo, and which side come next belong in the note you read before you press start, or in your own mouth during the rest. The timer's sentence is the name of the block.

## Count the last three seconds, and split long efforts in half

The second cue is a countdown you can hear: three, two, one. It is a run-up. You finish the rep you are on and meet the next phase on time, instead of discovering the change after it has already happened. Peak Interval's voice countdown does this at the end of an interval. Leave it on for simple same-move sessions and for circuits. It is useful even when you do not need the exercise name.

The third cue is a word in the middle of a long effort. A 20-second interval does not need one. A 60-second or 90-second block does, because the middle is where people ease off with nothing to aim at. Peak Interval speaks when a phase changes, so the reliable way to put a word at the halfway point is to split that work block into two equal phases with the same name. A 60-second squat becomes two 30-second phases, both called "squat". You hear the name again at 30 seconds, and you are still doing one exercise, not two. Skip the split on short rounds. A midpoint every 10 seconds is noise.

## Build it once, then leave the phone alone

Give the workout a preparation phase of about 10 seconds, so the first cue is not the instant you press start. Name every work interval. Name the rest interval too, if you want a spoken "rest" between moves rather than silence. Turn on the announcement for the next exercise and the spoken countdown for the last few seconds. Use the two-phase split only on the blocks that run about a minute or longer.

Save the workout. The words stay attached to the phases, and the next session starts without you retyping labels in the warm-up.

Test it the way you will train. Start the timer, put the phone where you cannot read it, and run one round with your eyes on the movement. You should hear the next exercise before the rest ends, and the countdown before the work begins. If you had to look, that phase is missing a name.

When the room is loud, add the Apple Watch tap at each change. The haptic does not say the exercise. It tells you the voice just spoke, which is enough if you were waiting for the word. If the session has to stay quiet, use the vibration-only setup in the [silent interval timer](/blog/posts/silent-interval-timer-vibration-only) guide instead of turning the voice up. If a playlist is swallowing the words, the [interval timer with music](/blog/posts/interval-timer-with-music) setup is the one that sorts the two audio streams.

## Leave these three on

Speak the next exercise whenever the moves change. Speak the last three seconds on every interval. Speak a halfway word only when the work block is long enough to drift, about a minute or more. A beep can still mark the edge of the phase. It does not have to carry the name.

You are done when you can finish the session without picking the phone up. The voice said what changed. You did the work.
