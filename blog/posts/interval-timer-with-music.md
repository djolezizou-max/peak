You queue up the playlist, start a 40/20 circuit, and thirty seconds in you realise you have no idea which phase you are in. The beep either never arrived or landed underneath a chorus and you worked straight through your rest. So you glance at the phone every round, which defeats the point of a timer.

Or the opposite happens. Every transition your music drops out completely, you get a second of silence, and the track limps back in a beat late. Twelve rounds of that and the workout feels like it is being interrupted by its own timer.

Both problems come from the same place, and neither is really about picking better songs. It is about how two apps share one audio output.

## Why the beep disappears — or your music does

Phones handle simultaneous audio in one of three ways, and every timer app picks one. It can **mix**, playing the cue on top of your music at full volume. It can **duck**, dropping the music by roughly 20 dB for the length of the cue so the beep sits on top. Or it can **interrupt**, pausing the music entirely and handing playback back afterwards.

Mixing is why you miss cues. A 200-millisecond beep at the same level as a dense mix is genuinely hard to hear, especially when a track is compressed and loud in the same frequency range. Interrupting is why your music stutters — and worse, some streaming apps do not reliably resume, so you finish a round to silence.

Ducking is the behaviour you want for interval work, and it is what most well-built timers use. If your current app stops your music dead every round, that is a design choice by that app, not something you can fix in settings. That is the moment to switch timers rather than switch playlists.

## Make the cue something a mix cannot swallow

Even with ducking on, a single short beep is the weakest possible signal. Two changes fix most of it.

First, use **distinct cues for different phases**. If work-start and rest-start make the same sound, you are relying on counting rather than listening, and counting is the first thing to go at round nine. In Peak Interval you set separate sounds for work, rest and preparation, so the cue tells you *what* changed, not just *that* something did.

Second, turn on the **voice countdown**. A spoken "three, two, one" is a full second of audio in the vocal frequency range, which is exactly where your ear is most sensitive and where a ducked instrumental has the most headroom. It is far harder to miss than a single tone, and it gives you a run-up rather than a surprise — you start the sprint on time instead of a half-second after it.

If you train with a podcast or an audiobook rather than music, voice cues get confusing fast. Stick to distinct tones there.

## Earbuds, gym speakers, and the channel music cannot drown

Where the sound comes out changes everything. On **earbuds**, ducking works properly and a moderate cue volume is plenty — you can usually run cues quieter than you think and still catch every one.

On a **gym or room speaker**, your phone's own speaker is the problem: it is competing with music that is louder and closer to everyone else than it is to you. If you are running a class or a garage session off a Bluetooth speaker, route the timer through that same speaker so both share the ducking, rather than leaving the phone on a bench trying to shout over it.

And if you wear an Apple Watch, use the **haptic taps**. A tap on the wrist is the one channel a playlist physically cannot cover, and it is the reason the Watch flow is worth setting up even when your phone is running the workout. For loud gyms, haptics as the primary signal and audio as the backup is more reliable than any volume setting.

## A setup worth two minutes before your next session

Give yourself a **preparation phase of at least 10 seconds**. That is enough to hit play on the playlist, get the earbuds seated, and be in position before the first work interval — instead of starting the timer, fumbling with music, and losing the first round.

Then run one honest test round. Start your usual playlist at your usual volume, run a single 40/20 with your cues on, and check whether you heard both transitions with your eyes closed. If you missed either, raise the cue volume or switch that phase to a voice cue and test again. Two minutes now beats guessing for the next twenty workouts.

The goal is not to hear the timer over the music. It is to stop looking at your phone. If you can run a full circuit by ear and by wrist alone, the setup is right — and the playlist gets to do the job it was there for.
