The set says 4x100 on 1:45. That is four hundreds, and you leave the wall again one minute and forty-five seconds after you left it the last time. A pace clock makes the departure obvious: you remember where the hand was, and you push off when it comes back. Your pool does not have one, so the leave time lives in your head, and by the third hundred you are guessing.

A phone on the deck does not replace the clock. You will not hear a speaker with your face in the water, and you are not climbing out to check. You need the one fact the pace clock was for — it is time to leave — on the wrist that is already in the pool.

## A send-off is a departure, not a swim time

A send-off is the time between departures, not the time you hope the swim takes. [Blueseventy](https://www.blueseventy.com/blogs/stories/beginner-swimmers-add-variety-to-your-workouts) uses a plain example: 5x100 on 1:45 means five hundreds, and you leave the wall for a new one every minute and forty-five seconds. [Sport Speed Lab](https://sportspeedlab.com/understanding-swim-terms/) states the rule that goes with it. You leave at the same point each repeat, and any time left until that point is rest. Swim the hundred faster and the rest grows. Swim it slower and the rest shrinks. The departure stays where you put it.

That is a different clock from a fixed rest. Fixed rest starts when you touch: finish the hundred, take the seconds you programmed, then go. If the hundred takes longer, you leave later. A send-off holds the leave time still and lets your swim decide the rest. [SG Sink or Swim](https://www.sgsinkorswim.com/post/mastering-the-distance-how-to-train-for-endurance-freestyle-events-using-interval-sets) shows the arithmetic on a 1:30 interval: a hundred swum in 1:20 leaves ten seconds at the wall.

Counting those seconds after you touch is how the set slips. You finish, you breathe, and "about fifteen" becomes whatever your head says. The next hundred starts when the counting gives up, which is not the send-off you wrote down.

## Set the send-off as one repeating phase

Do not split 1:45 into a swim phase and a rest phase. A beep at 1:20 means nothing useful if you are still mid-length, and it does not mean "leave." This set has one cue, and the cue is the departure.

Build one phase of 1:45 with no rest, and repeat it four times. That is the same shape as a [repeating interval with no rest phase](/blog/posts/30-second-interval-timer): every boundary means one thing. Here the thing is "go." Name the phase "100" so a glance at the wall shows the distance rather than a blank countdown. Put ten or fifteen seconds of prep in front, long enough to set your feet before the first leave. The prep is not a warm-up. Swim the easy lengths before you start the set.

The next line on the plan, 4x50 on :55, is a second phase with its own length and its own four rounds, in the same workout. You should not have to get out and rebuild the timer between them.

In Peak Interval that saved workout is a short prep, a 1:45 phase repeated four times, then the next send-off if the plan has one. The round count is the memory the set still needs. Which hundred you are on is the number that goes first once you are breathing hard.

An [EMOM](/blog/posts/how-to-run-an-emom-workout) is the same kind of window — leftover time is rest — and a different question. An EMOM sizes reps so the work fits inside one minute on the gym floor. A send-off sizes a departure for a distance, and the window is the interval on the plan, not sixty seconds.

## Feel the leave on your wrist

Start the workout on the Apple Watch and leave the phone on the deck. Once the session is on the Watch, the countdown and the tap at each phase run there. Before you put the phone down, confirm the workout is actually on the wrist. The short checklist is in [using an Apple Watch interval timer without your phone](/blog/posts/apple-watch-interval-timer-without-phone): open that workout on the Watch while the phone is still beside you, so a missed sync does not show up after you are wet.

A speaker on a kickboard is the wrong channel. The pool is loud, your ears are full of water, and the moment that matters is the leave, not a walk back to a screen. Peak Interval taps at the phase change. That tap is the pace clock. Snug the band before you get in. If a normal tap vanishes once you are moving, set Haptics to Prominent. You will not get a spoken round count with your head under. Look once, at the wall, if you need the number. The next tap is the signal the set depends on.

## When the hundred runs long

If you are still swimming when the tap fires, that repeat had no rest. The clock did not wait. The next departure is the next tap, one send-off later. Leaving the instant you touch, so you can chase the cycle you just missed, is a choice you can make at the wall. The timer will not build you a new interval to make up for it. A clock that restarts when you finish is a fixed-rest set, and that is the other workout.

If the hundred keeps running past the tap, the send-off was tight. Finish the repeat, take whatever rest is left until the next leave, and add time to the phase before the next session. Five seconds is enough to save and try. Changing the interval in your head, mid-set, is how 1:45 turns into "about two minutes."

A metronome under the cap marks stroke rate. It does not know that this hundred was supposed to start on 1:45.

## Leave on the tap

4x100 on 1:45 is four departures, a minute and forty-five seconds apart. It is not four swims plus a rest you time yourself. One phase of 1:45, four rounds, a few seconds of prep, and a tap on the wrist you swim with. The seconds you spend on the wall are the rest, and they stay honest because you stopped being the clock.
