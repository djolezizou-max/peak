You drop down, go until you can't, and get 31. Two weeks later you get 27, then 36. Either your strength is swinging wildly or the test is, and it is almost always the test: a different depth, a longer pause at the top, a different stopping point.

A push-up AMRAP only tells you something if it runs the same way every time. That comes down to three decisions you make once and then stop thinking about: how the set ends, what counts as a rep, and what you write down afterwards.

## Fixed window or to failure

A fixed window means as many reps as possible in a set time, usually one or two minutes. To failure means no clock limit, and the set ends when you break a rule you set beforehand. Both are legitimate tests, and they measure slightly different things.

Choose a fixed window if you want the easiest number to compare. The clock ends the set, so nobody has to judge whether that last shaky rep counted or whether you could have kept going. One minute rewards speed and suits most people. Two minutes rewards pacing as well, and it is the length the US Army's old fitness test used, so plenty of standards tables are built around it. If you top out under about 30 reps, a two-minute window adds little, because you will reach failure before the clock does.

Choose to failure if you stop well short of a minute, or if you care about strength endurance more than speed. It needs a harder stop rule, because failure is a judgement call. A workable one: the set ends at the first rep you cannot complete to standard, or the first time you rest at the top for longer than two seconds. Knees touching the floor also ends it.

## Decide what counts as a rep

Write the standard down and test against it every time. Three things need settling: depth, lockout and body line. Depth can be chest to the floor, chest to a fist, or upper arms below parallel. Any of them works, but pick one, because a few centimetres of depth can be worth several reps.

Lockout means elbows straight at the top before the next rep starts. Body line means hips and shoulders rising together, with no worm off the floor. When a rep misses any of these it does not count, and you keep going if the clock is still running.

Depth is the part that drifts when you are tired, so make it checkable. A rolled towel or yoga block under your chest gives you something to touch, and filming the set from the side once a month shows you what fatigue does to your form. The reps you lose by getting stricter are not a setback. They are the number you should have been recording all along.

Rest at the top in a fixed-window test is your call, as long as your hands and feet stay put. Pausing is usually how the last 20 seconds gets done, and it costs you time rather than ending the set.

## Set the timer so nothing else changes

Counting reps takes all the attention you have, so the clock should need none of it. For a one-minute test you need a short prep phase and a single 1:00 work phase with a clear end signal. Fifteen seconds of prep gets you from the phone to the floor with your hands set, and that same 15 seconds every test removes one more small variable.

For a two-minute test, split the work into two 1:00 phases with no rest between them. You get a cue at the halfway point, which is your pacing check. Glance at your count there and you will learn more from it than from the final score: 40 in the first minute and 8 in the second is a very different set from 26 and 22.

For a to-failure test, set one work phase far longer than you will last, such as five minutes, and stop when you break your rule. The time left on the clock tells you how long the set lasted, which is worth logging next to the reps.

## Log it so week six means something

Record the date, the protocol, the rep standard, the reps, and the first-minute split if you ran two minutes. Add one note on context, such as "after legs yesterday" or "6 am, no coffee". A line like "1:00, chest to towel, 34 reps, fresh" can be compared with next month's. A line that just says "34" can't.

Test under the same conditions each time. Same time of day, same warm-up, same point in the week, and first thing in the session rather than after other work. Keep incline and knee push-ups as separate lines in your log, because a jump from one variation to another is a change of test rather than progress.

Testing weekly is fine, since one set costs little recovery, but judge the trend rather than any single result. A swing of two or three reps between tests is usually just a good or bad day. Look at the last three results against the three before them, and treat a real change as one that holds.

The number is also useful for setting your training. A common way to build on it is an EMOM of push-ups at around half your test score: if you tested at 30, do 15 reps at the top of every minute for ten minutes, which is 150 quality reps with no failed sets. When the last round stops feeling hard, retest. ## The short version

Pick one protocol: a one-minute window for most people, two minutes if you are past about 30 reps and want a pacing test, or to failure with a written stop rule if you don't last a minute. Fix the depth with something you touch, let the timer run the same prep and work phases every time, and log the protocol with the score.

Then keep it boring. The test is only as useful as it is repeatable, and three matching results will tell you more than one big number.
