You finished twenty minutes of EMOM, your heart rate spent most of it above 160, and you are now trying to work out whether to write it in the box marked HIIT or the box marked strength. Nobody at the gym agrees. Half the internet says EMOM is HIIT by definition, the other half says it is conditioning, and both sides say it with total confidence.

The confusion is not your fault. EMOM and AMRAP are CrossFit vocabulary, invented in gyms to describe how a session is scored. HIIT is lab vocabulary, invented to describe what a session does to your cardiovascular system. They are two different kinds of word, so asking whether one is the other only has an answer once you decide which definition you are using — and that decision quietly changes how you should set your timer.

## HIIT is defined by the recovery, not the format

The research definition of high-intensity interval training is repeated bouts of near-maximal effort separated by periods of recovery that are genuinely lower intensity. Both halves matter. Effort alone is not HIIT — a hard continuous twenty-minute run is just a hard continuous run. The interval structure is the point, and the recovery is what makes the next hard bout possible.

That is why the useful test is not "was it hard" but "did the work stop long enough for the next bout to be run at the same intensity." In a Tabata that is 20 seconds on and 10 off. In a Norwegian 4x4 it is four minutes hard and three easy. In both cases the rest is written into the protocol and you cannot accidentally skip it.

EMOM does not write the rest in. It writes the *start* in. That is the whole difference, and everything else follows from it.

## So an EMOM is HIIT only if you sized the reps for it

Every minute on the minute means a new set starts at 0:00 regardless of how long the last one took. Your rest is whatever the minute has left over. Pick ten burpees that take you 50 seconds and you have bought yourself 10 seconds of standing up — that is not a recovery period, that is a pause, and by minute six you are working continuously at whatever pace your body can actually sustain. Intensity falls, and what you did was a moderate continuous effort with a stutter in it.

Pick five burpees that take 20 seconds and you get 40 seconds back. Now each set can genuinely be run near-maximal, the heart rate spikes and partially recovers twelve or twenty times, and you have done something the literature would recognise as interval training.

The rough line is a work-to-rest ratio around 1:1 or better. Under roughly 30 seconds of work in the minute, your EMOM behaves like HIIT. Push past 45 and it stops. Nothing about the format changed — only the rep count did, which is why two people can do "the same" EMOM and be honestly describing two different training stimuli.

## AMRAP is a different question with a cleaner answer

AMRAP — as many rounds as possible in a fixed window — has no prescribed rest at all. A 12-minute AMRAP is a single continuous effort where you are the one deciding when to breathe, and the scoring actively punishes you for deciding often. It is a time trial.

That makes AMRAP the clearest no of the three. It is excellent conditioning, it will leave you on the floor, and it is not interval training, because there are no intervals in it. If you are logging training to make sure you get two or three genuine HIIT sessions a week, AMRAPs should not be what fills that quota.

Tabata is the opposite end: 20 seconds of all-out work, 10 seconds of rest, eight rounds. It is HIIT by construction and there is no way to size the reps that breaks it.

## Set the timer for what you actually want

This is where the label stops being trivia. If you want the EMOM to be HIIT, do not set a 20-minute countdown and count your own minutes — set it up as repeating intervals so the work and rest phases are separate and audible. In Peak Interval that means 20 or 30 seconds of work, the balance as rest, repeated twenty times, so the cue that tells you to stop is as loud as the one that tells you to start.

A plain minute-repeating timer gives you one beep per minute and leaves the rest invisible. That sounds like a small difference until minute fourteen, when the only thing keeping your rest honest is a signal you cannot hear because you never set one. Splitting the phase makes the ratio you designed the ratio you actually train.

If instead you want the strength-biased version — heavy sets, longer work, short pauses — then a minute-marker timer is the right tool and you should stop worrying about whether it counts as HIIT. It does not, and that is fine. It is doing a different job.

## What to write in your log

Count an EMOM toward your weekly HIIT target when the work took half a minute or less and you could hit the same output on the last round as the first. Count it as conditioning or strength when the work ate most of the minute. If you are unsure which one you did, the honest tell is whether rounds eighteen through twenty looked like rounds one through three.

Then set the timer to match the answer rather than the name. The format is a scoring convention; the work-to-rest ratio is the training. Change the ratio and you have changed what the session does, whatever the whiteboard called it.
