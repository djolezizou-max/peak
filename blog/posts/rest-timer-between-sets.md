Your programme says three minutes between heavy sets. You take three minutes on the first one, then you check a message, then someone asks how many you have left, and the fourth set goes in at six and a half minutes. The session was written to take fifty minutes and it took eighty.

Rest is the only part of a lifting session that nobody times honestly. The reps get counted, the weight gets logged, and the gap in between — the variable that actually decides whether this is strength work or a long conversation with weights nearby — runs on vibes.

## The drift is bigger than you think it is

If you have never timed rest, the number that surprises people is not the average. It is the spread. Set one to set two is usually close to what you intended. By set four, when the working weight is heavy enough to be unpleasant, rest quietly doubles.

That is not laziness, it is the obvious response to a hard set: you sit down until you feel ready, and "ready" arrives later each time. The problem is that feel-based rest makes the session non-repeatable. Next week you do the same weights with different recovery and you have no idea whether you got stronger or just rested longer.

Timing it fixes that in one step. You are not aiming for military precision — you are aiming to know that both sessions ran on the same clock.

## Pick the number from what the set is for

Rest length is not a personality trait, it is a function of what the set is trying to do. Three brackets cover almost everything:

**Heavy strength work — 3 to 5 minutes.** Sets of one to five near your top end. Phosphocreatine takes several minutes to come back, and cutting rest here mostly reduces the weight you can lift, which is the one thing you were training.

**Hypertrophy work — 90 seconds to 2 minutes.** Sets of six to twelve. Long enough that the next set is not sabotaged, short enough to keep the session dense.

**Accessory and conditioning work — 30 to 60 seconds.** Curls, face pulls, calf raises, anything where fatigue is part of the point.

Superset the small stuff and the rest takes care of itself. It is the heavy end of the session that needs a clock, because that is exactly where the drift happens.

## Why a stopwatch is the wrong tool for this

Most people time rest with the stock stopwatch, and it fails in a specific way: it counts up, silently, and asks you to keep watching it. You have to remember to start it, remember to look at it, and remember what number you were waiting for. Miss any of those and you have a stopwatch reading 4:12 and no idea whether that was the plan.

A countdown with an audio or haptic cue removes all three jobs. You start it as the bar hits the rack and you stop thinking about rest until something tells you the set is due. That is the entire mechanic, and it is why a proper interval timer beats a stopwatch even for something as simple as one number repeated ten times.

In Peak Interval you build this as a rest phase with a round count — three minutes, eight rounds — and the cue fires at the end of each one. On Apple Watch it is a tap on the wrist, so it works with the phone in a bag and headphones playing something loud. That matters more than it sounds: the whole reason rest drifts is that the phone came out.

## Time the work too, if the work is timed

Plenty of lifting sessions have a timed component sitting inside them — a 40-second carry, a plank between squat sets, a rower finisher. If you are already running a rest timer, the natural move is to build the work and the rest as one alternating block rather than juggling two apps.

That is the setup a lot of people go looking for and cannot find: one timer that handles a work period and a rest period together, repeated for the number of sets, and does not need to be re-entered every session. Build it once, save it, and next week it opens as a preset.

The version worth having also lets the two phases differ per block, so a session can run four sets of heavy squats at three minutes rest, then drop straight into 40 on / 20 off accessory work without stopping the timer and starting a different one.

## Do not let the timer become the workout

One caveat, because rigid rest can be worse than sloppy rest. If the timer says go and your heart rate is still at 150 and your grip has not returned, taking another thirty seconds is the correct call. The number is a default, not a rule, and on a bad day the defaults should move.

The point of timing rest is not obedience, it is information. Once you know that you actually took four minutes rather than two, you can decide whether that was the session drifting or the session needing it. Without the clock you were never making that decision at all.

Start with one thing: pick a number for your heaviest lift of the day, run it as a countdown with a cue you can feel, and leave everything else alone. The session gets shorter by about fifteen minutes, and it is the same session.
