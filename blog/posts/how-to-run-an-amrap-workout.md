AMRAP is one line of instructions. As many rounds as possible of a short circuit before the clock runs out, and your score is however far you got. Twelve minutes, ten push-ups, fifteen squats, five burpees, go.

The line hides a pacing decision that most people get wrong in the first three minutes. The clock is fixed and the volume is not, so every second you spend sprinting round one gets paid back with interest on rounds four through eight. If your AMRAPs feel like a fast start followed by a long collapse, the format is not the problem. The pace is.

## Pick the time cap before you pick the movements

The time cap decides what kind of workout you are doing, so choose it first. Five to seven minutes is a hard, near-redline effort where you can push from the start. Ten to fifteen minutes is the standard range and rewards a pace you could hold for twice as long. Twenty minutes and up is closer to a steady aerobic piece with a score attached.

Then build a circuit where one round takes roughly 60 to 90 seconds fresh. That gives you eight to twelve rounds in a twelve-minute cap, which is enough repetitions of the circuit that pacing matters and your score means something. A round that takes four minutes turns a twelve-minute AMRAP into three rounds and a partial, and a single bad transition swings the result by a third.

Keep the movements ones you can do without thinking under fatigue. AMRAP punishes technical lifts that fall apart when you are breathing hard, because the format quietly encourages you to rush them.

## Pace for the last round, not the first

The most useful number in an AMRAP is your round time. Before you start, decide what it should be: if you want ten rounds in twelve minutes, every round needs to land at about 72 seconds. Then hold that from the first round, even though round one at 72 seconds will feel almost insultingly easy.

That easy feeling is the point. Round one at 55 seconds does not bank seventeen seconds. It costs you about a minute spread across the back half of the workout, because you spend it taking unplanned rests on the burpees. The people with the best scores have the flattest round times, not the fastest first round.

A simple check is to break the cap into thirds. At the four-minute mark you should be right on target, at eight minutes no more than one round behind, and the final four minutes is where you are allowed to push. If you are ahead at four minutes, you went out too hard and should settle down now, not later.

## Score it as rounds plus reps, every time

The standard AMRAP score is completed rounds plus the extra reps into the unfinished round, written as 9+17. Use that format every time. A score of "about nine rounds" cannot be compared with next month's attempt, and comparing is the only reason to score at all.

Count rounds out loud or with a tally, not in your head. By minute nine, almost nobody can reliably say whether they are on round seven or eight. A strip of tape and a marker, a stack of poker chips you move one at a time, or a round counter on your wrist all work. Memory does not.

Write down the circuit, the cap and the score together. "12 min: 10 push-ups, 15 squats, 5 burpees, 9+17" is a benchmark you can repeat in six weeks and genuinely learn something from.

## Set the timer up as one countdown with checkpoints

An AMRAP does not need intervals. It needs one long work phase, the length of your cap, with a prep phase in front so your hands are on the floor before the clock starts. Fifteen seconds of prep is enough to get from the phone to the first push-up.

What makes the timer actually useful is checkpoints. Split the cap into equal phases instead of one block, three four-minute phases for a twelve-minute AMRAP, and you get a cue at each third without doing any arithmetic mid-workout. That cue is your pacing check: on target, behind, or ahead and needing to ease off. In Peak Interval that is three work phases with no rest between them, saved once and reused, and the cue that starts the last phase doubles as your signal to empty the tank.

Run it from your wrist if you can. With a circuit spread across the floor, a phone propped on a bench is one more thing to walk to and squint at. A tap at each checkpoint and a spoken cue you can hear over your own breathing lets you keep your eyes on the next movement.

## Bottom line

Choose the cap first, build a round that takes 60 to 90 seconds, and set a target round time you can hold from the first minute to the last. Score it as rounds plus reps and write it down with the circuit.

Let the timer split the cap into checkpoints so you know at four and eight minutes whether you are on pace. Most of a good AMRAP score comes from not giving time away in the first three minutes.
