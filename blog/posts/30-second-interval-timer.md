You want something simple: a cue every 30 seconds, for the next twenty or thirty minutes, without looking at your wrist or tapping a screen. Change position, change side, change movement — whatever the 30 seconds is for, you just need to know when it lands.

What you find instead is a stock timer that fires once and then sits there waiting to be dismissed. So you dismiss it, restart it, and do that sixty times in half an hour. That is not a timer problem you can fix with better discipline. It is a category difference between a countdown and an interval.

## A countdown timer and an interval timer are not the same tool

The timer built into your phone and watch is a **countdown**. It runs to zero, alerts, and stops. Some versions keep buzzing until you acknowledge them, which is worse than useless mid-set — the alert does not clear itself, so you have to interact with the device to make it stop, and the next 30 seconds has already started by the time you do.

An **interval timer** loops. It runs a phase, fires a cue at the boundary, and immediately starts the next phase with no input from you. That single behavioural difference is the whole reason a dedicated app exists for something a stopwatch appears to already do.

The practical test is easy: start the timer, put your hands on the floor, and see whether it reaches minute five without you. If it needs a tap, it is a countdown wearing a timer's name.

## Build a 30 second cadence that runs to the end of the session

There are two ways to express "every 30 seconds," and they behave differently.

The first is a **single 30-second work phase with no rest, repeated for N rounds**. Sixty rounds gets you 30 minutes. Every boundary sounds the same, which is exactly right when the cue means "switch" and nothing else — alternating sides on a plank, rotating through a five-station circuit, changing lead leg on lunges.

The second is **30 seconds work paired with a rest phase**, which is what you want if the cue needs to mean two different things. 30/30 for twenty rounds is a twenty-minute session where each boundary tells you whether to go or stop. In Peak Interval you set the work and rest sounds separately, so you never have to count rounds to know which phase you are in — the cue itself tells you.

Pick the first if 30 seconds is a metronome. Pick the second if 30 seconds is a work bout. Building the wrong one is the most common reason a session feels like it is fighting you at round twelve.

## Odd totals, and why "eight rounds" is the wrong default

A lot of timer presets quietly assume Tabata: 20/10, eight rounds, four minutes, done. So when you want 47 rounds of 30 seconds because that is what your circuit works out to, a preset-driven app makes you fight it.

Set the round count to the number you actually need. If your session is 22 minutes of 30-second switches, that is 44 rounds — not 40 because it is rounder, and not eight because the app suggested it. And if you want the cadence to run open-ended while you decide when to stop, set the rounds high and end the session yourself rather than letting the timer's arithmetic decide the length of your workout.

Add a **preparation phase** at the front, too. Ten or fifteen seconds before the first cue is the difference between starting the session and scrambling into position while the first 30 seconds burns.

## Save it once so you never build it twice

The setup above takes about forty seconds. Doing it every training day is forty seconds of friction on the thing you are trying to make frictionless, and it is the point where most people go back to the stopwatch.

Save the 30-second cadence as a named workout — "30s switches, 44 rounds" — and starting tomorrow's session is one tap. Peak Interval keeps saved workouts on the Apple Watch as well, so the session starts from your wrist and the haptics fire there, which is the version of this that genuinely never needs a screen.

That is the real goal. Not a timer with more features, but a session where after the first tap the device is silent about everything except the boundary you asked it to mark.

## The short version

Build a 30-second work phase, no rest, with the round count set to your actual session length rather than a preset. Add a short preparation phase, set the cue to something you can hear or feel over what you are doing, and save it under a name you will recognise next week.

Then start it and forget it exists. If you find yourself reaching for the device before the session is over, the setup is wrong — not your attention span.
